## 01 · Context

**TPremia is Grupo Salinas' rewards program: it recognizes customers and gives them benefits for purchases, credit, money transfers, and every transaction they make. The challenge: four brands that look nothing alike under one system.**

Grupo Salinas is one of Mexico's largest conglomerates. I joined to lead the design of TPremia, its loyalty program, for four of its key brands: Banco Azteca, Elektra, Presta Prenda, and ITALIKA.

The business goal was clear: a cross-brand program where customers earn T points from their transactions and redeem them for rewards (from movie tickets to discounts and products). The design challenge was harder. A bank and a department store don't behave the same way, don't share the same customers, or the same actions that create value. We had to unify them without erasing what makes each brand unique.

And all of it for a tough audience: users with low digital literacy—older people, with low socioeconomic and educational levels. Zero margin for confusing interfaces.

---

## 02 · My role

**Lead Product Designer. From solo design to handoff with each brand's teams.**

I worked as Lead Product Designer, solo during the design phase. Once the system was defined, I shared all the work with each brand's design teams so they could permeate it through their own teams.

My role wasn't just designing screens. I communicated project findings and my hypotheses about friction points users might face to the Product Owner. Designing for this audience meant anticipating where they would get stuck, before it happened.

---

## 03 · What we built

**A site to join the program, and an app to live the rewards.**

TPremia had two major zones. The first, the access site: registration (with required data, terms acceptance, and SMS verification), login, and recovery flows for UID and password via token. The second, the internal app: a rewards catalog and a viewer where users see their accumulated T points, their level, and the benefits they're unlocking.

![arquitectura-grupo-salinas.png#compact](/images/projects/grupo-salinas/arquitectura-grupo-salinas.png)

The home showed the essentials at a glance: how many points you have, how much you need for your next reward, and your level progress. All supported by a progress bar and "wow" moments (like a tenure points bonus) to reinforce the feeling of advancement.

## 04 · Designing for the hardest user

**When your user has never used an app, every interaction counts.**

The target audience was the most demanding possible for a digital product: people with little or no digital experience. That forced us to think through every interaction and microinteraction with one criterion: absolute clarity.

I kept all on-platform information as explicit as possible, supported by clear iconography that pointed to the concrete actions users needed to take. Nothing implicit, nothing taken for granted. If the user had to do something, the design told them with words and icons—not conventions they might not know.

## 05 · The key decision — redesign the model, not just the screen

**Banco Azteca was the hardest brand. And the important decision there wasn't UI—it was business.**

Banco Azteca was the first brand I worked on, and the most complex. The original model assumed users would register their account on the platform and link it to their card number, so every banking operation (a deposit, a withdrawal, checking balance) would generate points automatically.

I saw a root problem. For this user, registering their bank account on an online platform was a huge trust barrier. The model assumed a level of digital comfort this audience didn't have. I raised it with the Product Owner.

The solution for phase 1 redesigned the entire flow. Instead of linking the bank account, the user registered and got their own app account number. When making an operation at the counter, they showed their webapp (where their number appeared) or a downloadable image, and the teller recorded the operation, generating the points. Trust was preserved: nobody had to enter sensitive banking data online.

For the other brands (product or service sales), the flow was more direct: each transaction generated points, showing the same user number or image. But Banco Azteca set the pattern for how to solve the trust friction without sacrificing the business goal.

Banco Azteca's best design wasn't a screen. It was changing how the user earned points, so they felt safe doing it.

## 06 · Four systems, one root

**Four separate Design Systems, built on the same foundations.**

Each brand launched its own version of the product: different but recognizable as part of the same project. To support that, I built four Design Systems, one per brand.

Why four and not one? Each brand had real differences: colors, spacing, typography. And in 2020 Figma tokens didn't exist yet (they arrived in 2023), so the only way to give each brand its own identity was a separate file.

But separate didn't mean disconnected. I designed all four systems sharing the same structural foundations. Even though the files weren't technically linked, it was clear they belonged to the same project. That decision—made by instinct for scalability before having the tool to automate it—optimized the entire process.

![grupo-salinas-design-system.png](/images/projects/grupo-salinas/grupo-salinas-design-system.png)

After two years of operation, the programs unified into a single loyalty system for all brands, simplifying the customer experience and strengthening cross-brand loyalty at Grupo Salinas. That unification validated the initial bet: the systems already shared a root, so bringing them together was evolution, not reconstruction.
