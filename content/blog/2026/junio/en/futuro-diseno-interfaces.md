# Every app looks the same. That's about to change.

We've been stuck in the same place for almost a decade.

Flat design won. Clean, fast, scalable. Perfect for design systems and large teams. But at some point it stopped being a decision and became the path of least resistance. Today 90% of apps look identical: white or almost-white background, blue or almost-blue button, a grid of identical cards, a safe sans-serif typeface. Technically there's nothing wrong with that. The problem is there's nothing left to look at.

Michal Malewicz said it recently in an article that made some noise: the industry has spent years confusing texture with trend.

![malewicz-ui-direction.png](/images/blog/malewicz-ui-direction.png)

*Image: Michal Malewicz - UI Design Direction 2026-2027 (Medium, May 2026). Source: [michalmalewicz.medium.com](https://michalmalewicz.medium.com/ui-design-direction-2026-2027-2b4b6eb88336). Used for reference purposes.*

Every time someone applies a new effect to an icon, they call it "morphism" and treat it like a new era of design. Disco-morphism, metal-morphism, squircle-morphism. Most of them are just materials layered over the same old structure. A costume, not a language.

He's right about that. But I think he stopped short of something important.

We're not watching the end of empty trends. We're watching the first symptoms of something far more interesting.

---

## The technical barrier fell, and that changes everything

For years, experimenting in UI carried a high cost. Prototyping something unconventional took weeks. Convincing an engineering team to build it took months. And if it didn't work, the cost of failure was real. That bred a design culture that was conservative out of necessity, not conviction.

That changed.

Today a designer with judgment can prototype an entirely new interaction in hours. They can generate variations, iterate on material physics, explore behaviors that used to require an animation team. AI tools speed up the exploration without replacing the judgment. And that detail matters.

It's not that AI is designing for us. It's that AI took away our excuse not to experiment.

---

## Skeuomorphism 2.0 isn't nostalgia. It's physics.

There's one thing Malewicz gets right, and it's worth taking seriously: what's coming isn't an aesthetic revival of early-2000s skeuomorphism. No leather calculators, no spiral notebooks. It's something else.

It's the ability to calculate real physical behavior in real time.

A button that bends from the exact point where you touched it. A surface that casts light onto the element above it. Materials that respond to the device's orientation with reflections that come from the camera, not from a generic shader. That's not decoration. It's physical coherence inside a digital interface, and it changes how the brain processes the interaction.

The difference between the original skeuomorphism and what's coming is the same as the difference between a photo of a campfire and feeling the heat. One represents. The other responds.

---

## The era of borrowed rules is ending

Here's the point I find most relevant, and the one that rarely gets discussed head-on.

Interface design always borrowed its rules from other disciplines. From print. From architecture. From cognitive psychology. And that was fine. We needed to build on something while the industry matured.

But digital interfaces now have enough history of their own to start generating their own rules. Not rules adapted from another field, but principles born specifically from how humans interact with surfaces that are reactive, tactile, connected, and contextual.

That's what I think is coming: a generation of designers who don't ask "how do we adapt this from the physical world?" but "what's possible that only exists here?" Interfaces that don't imitate real materials but invent their own laws of behavior. Systems where physics is a design decision, not a constraint.

---

## The real risk isn't excess. It's mediocrity in a new costume.

There's an obvious trap in all this, and it's worth naming.

The same freedom that enables exploration can produce more elaborate slop. Effects with no purpose. Animations that distract. Materials that look impressive in a 15-second video and are unbearable after two minutes of real use. We're already seeing the first examples.

The difference between design that moves forward and design that just looks different was always the same: judgment. Solid fundamentals beneath the visual decisions. Knowing why something works before knowing how it looks.

What changes now is who gets to experiment. Before, only teams with resources could try things outside the norm. Today any designer with judgment can reach territory that used to require a team of ten.

That's what makes me optimistic. Not the textures. Not the morphisms. The democratization of exploration grounded in fundamentals.

---

## What's coming

I don't know exactly how interfaces will look in five years. Nobody knows for sure.

What I do believe: they'll look less alike. They'll have more personality, because the teams building them will have more tools to express intent. They'll behave in ways that look like gimmicks today and will be user expectations in three years.

And the designers who lead that transition won't be the ones who use the new tools best. They'll be the ones with enough judgment to tell when an exploration has substance and when it's just air inside a very well-rendered balloon.

An interesting era is coming. The best way to prepare isn't to chase trends faster. It's to build the judgment that lets you tell the ones that matter from the ones that don't.
